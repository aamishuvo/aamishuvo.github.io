Every approval-pipeline review I have ever seen starts the same way: pull the ticket counts per stage, sort descending, and declare the top of the list the bottleneck. It feels rigorous. It is usually wrong.

When I analysed two years of corporate document-approval records, the stages with the most tickets were the fast ones. High volume, quick turnaround — they looked busy because they *were* busy, processing efficiently. The genuinely damaging stages were quiet: few documents, but each one held for weeks.

## The reframe

Rank each stage by **delay-days held** — the sum of days documents sat waiting in that stage — instead of by how many documents passed through it. The league table inverts. Stages that never appeared in anyone's complaints turned out to be holding the majority of total delay.

The mechanism is simple once you see it. A stage that processes 500 documents in a day each contributes 500 delay-days a year. A stage that processes 20 documents but holds each for 25 days contributes 500 delay-days too — from 4% of the volume, and with none of the visibility.

## What changed

The PMO recommendations followed the delay rather than the noise: delegation thresholds on the quiet stages, escalation clocks that start automatically, and a weekly report of the ten oldest documents in the pipeline by *age*, not by department.

Turnaround time came down 42% year over year. Not because anyone worked faster — because we finally pointed the pressure at the stages where the time actually lived.

The general lesson travels well beyond approvals: **volume is what your process shows you; time is what your stakeholders feel.** Measure the one they feel.
